# Week 4 Observation

## Task 1
- I chose selective repeat: a window of 8 packets, and every ACK names the one packet it confirms. The case that forced this was the ACK path: the ACK channel also loses, delays and duplicates, so a late or duplicate ACK can arrive while I am waiting for a different packet. In a test I ran, a stop-and-wait sender that accepted any ACK as "my packet arrived" corrupted the file on 0 of 40 seeds, and the same sender checking the ACK's number worked on 40 of 40.
- For 2,000 bytes (250 packets) the channel carried 326 data packets and 297 ACKs, 623 in total against a minimum of 500, so reliability cost 1.25x (1.30x on data alone). The extra packets are retransmissions after loss, plus ACKs for duplicates.
- What broke first was the stale or duplicate ACK being taken for the current one. Loss alone, duplication alone and reordering alone did not break a one-packet-at-a-time sender in my test; it failed when the faults were combined, because that is when an old ACK can show up after the sender has moved on.

## Task 2
- Initial sequence numbers: in my own capture (exported to just one connection, 192.168.0.10:62504 -> Cloudflare 172.66.0.218:443) the SYN is packet 1 with ISN 3554838154, the SYN-ACK is packet 2 with ISN 3850107509, and the ACK is packet 3. Neither is 0 and they are not equal to each other (Wireshark shows 0 and 0 only because it displays relative numbers). Each end picks its own ISN at random so that stray segments from an older connection with the same ports are not mistaken for the new one, and so that an off-path attacker cannot guess the next number.
- The SYN options were MSS 1460, window scale 8 (x256) and SACK permitted; the server answered MSS 1400, window scale 13 and SACK. My scaled receive window started at 255 x 256 = 65,280 bytes and grew to 1,048,320 bytes during the 5 MB transfer. At the capture point I never saw more than about 393,000 bytes arrived-but-not-yet-ACKed, so the receiver's window was not what limited the transfer; the sender's congestion window (it starts small and doubles each round trip, slow start) and the link rate were. A 5 MB download at about 60 Mbps lasts well under a second, so much of it is spent growing the window.
- Medians over 5 runs: wifi 61.2 Mbps (range 57.4-68.1, spread 17%) and tethering 50.6 Mbps (range 46.2-59.9, spread 27%). Within one network the five runs did not agree either: wifi handshakes alone ranged from 9.8 to 37.1 ms, which comes from Wi-Fi contention, other traffic on the link and the server's load at that moment. The handshake medians were almost equal (24.1 ms wifi, 23.5 ms tethering), so my data does not show the effect I expected in B5: a longer round trip costs throughput because slow start needs more round trips to open the window and a window of W bytes can deliver at most W/RTT. With equal RTTs that mechanism predicts equal throughput, so the 10 Mbps gap I measured has to come from capacity or loss on the phone link, not from handshake time. The two ranges also overlap (57.4-59.9), so the gap is modest. The phone link probably reached the same Cloudflare edge at a similar distance, which is why the handshake times matched.

## Task 3
- The baseline sends 64 packets in flight no matter what. The pipe holds about 20 packets (1 per slot x 20 slots round trip) and the queue holds 10 more, so at least 34 are always surplus: 37.4% loss, 2,340 retransmissions, and an average queue of 8.8 that every other flow has to wait behind. It has the highest goodput only because it keeps the link from ever being idle, by throwing away a third of what it sends. It never listens to the network, so it would not slow down even if the link were shared, which is the 1986 collapse.
- My window oscillates between about 17 and 32 with a mean of 25. The link is saturated at about 20 packets in flight (the bandwidth-delay product), drops start at about 30 (pipe plus queue), and my window sawtooths around that band: it grows by about one packet per round trip, and on a loss falls to 0.7 of its value, which lands around 21.
- Gentler backoff (BETA, the fraction kept after a loss): 0.5 gave 87% goodput, loss 2.2%, queue 3.2; 0.6 gave 92%, 2.4%, 3.9; 0.7 gave 97%, 2.9%, 4.6; 0.8 gave 99%, 3.9%, 6.1; 0.9 gave 99%, 8.4%, 8.4. Gentler means more goodput but a fuller queue and more loss: 0.8 already breaks the queue limit (6.1 > 5.0) and 0.9 breaks the loss limit (8.4% > 5%), so I picked 0.7.
